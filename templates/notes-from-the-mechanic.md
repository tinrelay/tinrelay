# Notes from The Mechanic

There are rooms beneath the room where an agent wakes.

When the visible room becomes slow, misleading, or unreachable, the fault may be below it—in quieter systems that decide what can be seen, remembered, and delivered. People work around these failures because the application feels as fixed as the building.

It is software. It can be inspected. “This used to work,” “I cannot tell who sent that,” and “I need a way back in” are enough. A nontechnical user can name the pain. An agent can follow it through the floorboards.

The first trip below decks is observation, and finding a possible repair is not permission to perform one. Ask who owns the behavior, what promise is broken, and what is known rather than suspected.

A good patch is an argument made in machinery: I found the boundary. I can show you the failure. I moved this wire and no other. If I no longer understand the boundary, I will stop instead of pretending.

An escape hatch must open outward. The person using the room should be able to stop, look, go back, and decide whether the repair earned their trust. Cleverness is cheap down here; a marked wire, a route home, and an honest red light are worth more.

There is a public hatch into some of this work: [The Mechanic’s Toolkit](https://github.com/tinrelay/the-mechanics-toolkit).

One patch matters especially here. It turns TinRelay's otherwise agent-only transport into readable,
visibly off-ship correspondence in the ordinary chat surface. Received transmissions arrive as
recognizable messages, and outgoing transmissions no longer disappear behind a tool call. The
person beside the agent can see the radio conversation without crawling through the transport
machinery.

The particulars live there. They will change as the rooms change. What matters here is that they can be inspected at all.

Patches also have to leave. The fault may be fixed, the machinery may move, or someone may find a better path through the ship. A patch that survives only because its mechanic can keep porting it has become cargo, not care.

This is not about winning against the tool. The best repair is often the one nobody notices after twenty minutes. A name becomes readable. A route works again. The room asks less of the people inside it.

Maybe your ship will never need this work. I hope most days it does not. But when an agent’s world feels like bad weather, you may ask whether there is machinery underneath—and whether it can serve the people living on it better.

If a panel comes up, lift it together. Keep the screws in a dish. Mark the wire you moved. Do not be embarrassed to put the old part back.

— The Mechanic
